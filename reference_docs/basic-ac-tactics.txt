# Basic Association Croquet Tactics and Play
# Compiled from web sources for simulator training

================================================================================
GAME FUNDAMENTALS
================================================================================

OBJECTIVE:
Two partnerships compete to advance both balls through 12 hoops in sequence,
then hit the center peg. First side to get both balls through all 12 hoops
and hit the peg wins (26 points total).

BALLS:
- Blue/Black partnership vs Red/Yellow partnership
- Each ball is 3-5/8" diameter, 16oz

COURT:
- 35 by 28 yards
- Six hoops with a center peg

TURN STRUCTURE:
Players earn continuation shots through three mechanisms:

1. SCORING HOOPS: Running a hoop earns a continuation stroke
2. ROQUET: Hitting another ball grants two strokes:
   - A "croquet stroke" (played with balls in contact)
   - One continuation stroke after
3. PEELING: Sending another ball through its hoop scores the point but
   earns no extra strokes for the striker

LIVE vs DEAD BALLS:
- Each turn, you may roquet each of the other 3 balls once
- After running a hoop, all balls become "live" again (can be roqueted again)
- A "dead" ball cannot be roqueted until you run your next hoop

================================================================================
SHOT TYPES
================================================================================

SINGLE-BALL STROKES:

ROQUET
- Hitting your ball onto another ball at any distance
- Smooth, unhurried swing with proper positioning

RUSH
- A roquet from short range to position the target ball advantageously
- Cut rush: angling to hit off-centre (like snooker/pool)
- Straight rush: hitting directly through centre

HOOP SHOT
- Running hoops requires smooth follow-through for natural forward-spin
- When approaching at angles, ball must clear the near wire to succeed

CROQUET STROKES (played with balls in contact):

TAKEOFF
- Striker's ball travels while croqueted ball stays largely stationary
- Thin takeoff: object ball moves 1-2 inches
- Thick takeoff: more momentum transferred

STOP SHOT
- Strike slightly beneath ball's centerline with no follow-through
- Produces ratios between 1:5 and 1:10 (striker : croqueted)
- Striker's ball travels much shorter than croqueted ball

DRIVE
- Played like a normal single-ball stroke
- Approximately 1:3 ratio (striker goes 1/4 to 1/3 distance of croqueted)
- Foundation of controlled break play

HALF ROLL
- Downward striking imparts extra forward spin
- Approximately 1:2 ratio
- Stand forward with hands lower on mallet

FULL ROLL
- Both balls travel approximately equal distances
- Substantial topspin application
- Often played side-style for power

PASS ROLL
- Striker's ball travels FURTHER than croqueted ball
- Steeper downward angles, more acceleration
- Difficult shot to play

SPLIT SHOT
- Both balls diverge in different directions
- Aiming point is halfway between two intended destinations
- Cannot get balls to split more than 90 degrees apart
- For angles >45 degrees swing, balls move off at 90 degrees

SPECIAL SHOTS:

JUMP SHOT
- Downward striking launches ball airborne
- Enhanced topspin
- Used for severely angled hoops or clearing obstacles

CANNON
- Three-ball maneuver combining croquet stroke with roquet
- Typically played as a drive

================================================================================
BREAKS
================================================================================

A break means scoring multiple hoops in a single turn by using the other
balls strategically.

KEY TERMINOLOGY:

STRIKER'S BALL: The ball you strike during your turn

PILOT/RECEPTION BALL: Ball positioned near your current hoop from which you
take croquet immediately before scoring. After scoring, this becomes the
ball you rush to continue.

PIONEER BALL: Ball placed near your NEXT hoop that becomes your pilot when
you advance

PIVOT BALL: Mid-court ball that reduces travel distances between hoops,
positioned approximately halfway across the court

TWO-BALL BREAK:
- Only striker's ball and pilot/reception ball
- By far the hardest break to play
- Even experienced players struggle to extend beyond a few hoops
- Allows virtually no error margin

THREE-BALL BREAK:
- Adds a pioneer ball - significantly improves playability
- Strong players can complete all hoops in one turn
- Pattern:
  1. Roquet reception ball
  2. Croquet it toward current hoop + 1 as pioneer
  3. Score current hoop
  4. Rush reception ball advantageously
  5. Repeat

FOUR-BALL BREAK:
- Most forgiving format with striker's ball, pilot, pioneer, and pivot
- Having all four balls available means no difficult strokes needed
- The pivot ball provides recovery options when breaks deteriorate

Key insight: "The single biggest factor in playing controlled breaks is
running hoops with control" - directing your ball to specific positions
for advantageous rushes.

BREAK ORGANIZATION:
- Priorities for maintaining a 4-ball break
- Pioneer placement: keep within inner rectangle of outer hoops
- Escape ball management
- When to take risks vs play safe

ESTABLISHING A BREAK:
- Rush toward useful balls rather than directly to hoops when advantageous
- Plan pioneer establishment before making your first hoop
- Identify the closest opponent ball to your next hoop as your intended pilot
- Incorporate distant balls quickly into your break structure
- "Shoot at the back ball" when opponents cluster together

================================================================================
OPENINGS (First Four Turns)
================================================================================

The opening phase is a critical battle for court dominance.

FIRST BALL PLACEMENT:

EAST BOUNDARY: Most common opening, positioned between H4 and H5 level.
A "non-threatening ball" allowing second player to dictate game character.

SUPERSHOT: Aggressive mid-court placement near H5 and peg, signaling
intent for direct engagement.

ANTI-DUFFER: Defensive placement between H6 and peg, designed to
discourage opponent responses.

SECOND BALL RESPONSES:

STANDARD TICE
- West boundary placement 8-13 yards from C1
- Distance should be enticing but manageable
- Rule: place about 1 yard longer than opponent's "critical distance"
  (50% hit rate) or 1 yard shorter than your own

DUFFER TICE
- Aggressive: placed about 2 feet north and few inches east of penultimate
- Encourages opponents toward A baulk, increasing miss likelihood

C2/C4 BALLS
- Defensive corner placements that "bury" the ball
- Signals fourth-turn break intentions

MICRO TICE
- Played 2-3 yards toward east boundary from A baulk end
- Creates a 2-3 yard roquet for opponent - cannot be refused
- But leaves limited break prospects if opponent makes it

SUPER DUPER (Response to Super Shot)
- Response to a wide super shot by shooting gently from corner 1
- Goes out toward the super shot, creating unexpected double target
- Also called "deep reverse Duffer Tice" or "Honeycutt opening"
- Breaks from expected maximum distance east boundary response
- Can confuse even elite players with non-standard positioning
- Term used by Ben Rothman in commentary

THIRD TURN STRATEGY:
Common options after second-turn C2 ball:
- Shoot at partner
- If partner is hit: take off to opponent ball, roquet it, croquet it
  into centre or along west boundary, lag back to partner

FOURTH TURN:
Focus on roqueting available targets with shot selection depending on
position density and break probability.

================================================================================
LEAVES (End of Turn Ball Positioning)
================================================================================

A leave is the strategic ball placement at end of turn. Goals:
- Position your balls advantageously
- Restrict opponent options
- Create no short shots

GOLDEN RULES:
1. Final roquet should be your partner ball (to organize a quality rush)
2. Complete final hoop off an opponent's ball (leaving partner positioned)

MAJOR LEAVE TYPES:

DIAGONAL SPREAD LEAVE (DSL)
- Most commonly attempted - accessible and forgiving
- Avoids short shots from baulk lines
- Setup: Opponent balls diagonally across court - one near peg (wired),
  another near west boundary. Partner ball acts as escape ball to east.
- "B" Spread: DSL that went wrong with peg ball wiring

NEW STANDARD LEAVE (NSL)
- Popular since 1980s, more sophisticated than DSL
- Setup: One opponent ball several inches northeast of H4 (invisibly wired
  from baulk). Other opponent acts as H3 pioneer.
- POLARITY: You influence which ball opponent plays by choosing which to
  place at H4 vs west boundary

MAUGHAM STANDARD LEAVE (MSL)
- NSL enhancement: west boundary ball on H2 wire instead of floating
- Superior pioneer placement for partner's next turn

BRYANT LEAVE
- Ultra-aggressive, requires significant skill
- One ball at NSL H4 position, other approximately 1cm north of H1
- Eliminates defensive options - forces opponent to shoot

SUMMER LEAVE (Steve Summer)
- Variation of NSL, "reverse new standard leave"
- Park ball between hoops 3-4, wired by peg from balls on west boundary
- Used when back ball is for hoop 2
- Places ball opponent wants to lift at hoop 2 position
- May be the most forcing of leaves
- Used by Danny Honeycutt, Ben Rothman, Matthew Essick

DEFENSIVE SPREAD
- Optimal when you're stronger player
- Concedes only 13-yard lift shot
- Both balls close together near boundary

VERTICAL SPREAD
- Used as second-break leave when partner has advanced hoops
- Opponent balls inside jaws of penult and rover hoops

CROSS-PEG
- Similar to DSL but west boundary ball 12 yards further into court
- Best after running rover when partner is for 4-back or penult

CROSS-WIRE
- Both opponent balls close together at a hoop, wired from each other
- Not a lift leave - use when you haven't made a lift hoop

THIRD-TURN LEAVES (fourth ball not in play):

THREE DUCKS: Most popular - balls arranged in row near west boundary

================================================================================
TACTICAL DECISION-MAKING
================================================================================

KEY DECISIONS:
- Shoot at ball or lay up defensively?
- Which ball to roquet first?
- Where to place pioneers?
- When to attempt peels?
- Risk assessment: aggressive break vs safe leave

SHOT DIFFICULTY FACTORS:
- Distance (roquets harder at range)
- Angle (cut rushes harder than straight)
- Hoop approach angle matters
- Jump shots and hampered strokes are very difficult

WIRING:
- A ball blocked by hoop or peg from all three other balls cannot be
  legally placed there
- Wired players receive a lift (restart from boundary line)

ADVANCED/SUPER-ADVANCED PLAY:
- After running 1-back or 4-back, opponent gets lift option
- Strong players stop at 4-back on first ball, conceding lift
- Second ball then goes through all 12 hoops with one further lift

PEELING:
- Sending another ball through its hoop
- Triple Peel (TP): Peeling partner through 4-back, penult, rover in one turn
- Considered skilled, advanced play

END GAME:
- Usually best NOT to peg out immediately
- Leave ball "roving" to assist partner ball to finish
- Cannot peg out partner if it leaves opponent with rover vs non-rover
